## 1. Who killed Goliath: David or Elhanan?

**Recommended 30 · text-vs-text · Current status: Textual conflict**

Meets the requested status filter.

**Side A: David kills Goliath.** KJV: 1 Samuel 17:50.

**Side B: Elhanan kills Goliath in the Hebrew text.** KJV: 2 Samuel 21:19.
The KJV adds brother here; this side compares the Hebrew and old Greek translation recorded for the same passage.

**KJV context:** 1 Chronicles 20:5.

**60-second summary**

The account in First Samuel names David as the man who killed Goliath. Second Samuel names Elhanan in another account. Its KJV wording says he killed Goliath's brother. The Hebrew text and old Greek translation cited by the record do not include the words for brother there. Those words do appear in the matching passage in Chronicles, which the KJV follows. That difference makes the translation central to this debate. Comparing the KJV verses alone would hide part of the issue. The record does not decide which version preserves the earlier wording. It also leaves open whether two people shared a name or one person had two names. These editions cannot establish what every ancient copy says. They also do not reveal why the KJV translators chose their wording.

**What would settle it:** Compare the earlier wording and the people named; the record establishes neither from the editions it cites.

**Why it made the list:** A familiar David story makes a hidden translation choice easy to explore, with clear limits on the conclusion.

**Readability:** 129 summary words; FK 6.36; combined prose FK 6.7.

## 2. Who prompted David's count: God or Satan?

**Recommended 30 · text-vs-text · Current status: Textual conflict**

Meets the requested status filter.

**Side A: The Lord prompts the count.** KJV: 2 Samuel 24:1.

**Side B: Satan prompts the count.** KJV: 1 Chronicles 21:1.

**60-second summary**

David orders a count of Israel in both accounts. Second Samuel connects that action with the Lord's anger against Israel. First Chronicles instead names Satan as the one who prompts David. The contrast concerns the cause each account gives for the same action. A reader may propose that God allowed Satan to act as an agent. That would place the two causes at different levels within one explanation. Neither quoted verse states that combined account, however. The record therefore preserves the difference rather than treating that explanation as a fact. It also does not establish that either supernatural cause actually operated in history. Readers can compare what each narrator says without claiming access to the cause behind the event. The debate turns on stated causes and a proposed link between them.

**What would settle it:** Look for a stated link between divine permission and Satan; these verses do not supply that combined explanation.

**Why it made the list:** Two short passages invite debate about responsibility, permission and what an explanation adds to a text.
